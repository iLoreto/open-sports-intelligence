How We Read the Game

The scoreboard tells you what happened. Watching the game helps you understand what might happen next. The market price tells you whether that possibility is worth the risk.

We're developing a human-led research method for live sports markets. Not a magic engine, not automatic bets, and certainly not guaranteed winners.

Four sports. Different kinds of uncertainty.

🎾 Tennis

Watch the individual: serve percentage, return pressure, footwork, errors and fatigue. A player can struggle before the scoreboard reveals it—but they can recover too.

Research concept: Collapse Indicator

🏀 Basketball

Watch the five-player unit: spacing, cohesion, rotations and who gets minutes. A scoring prop depends on both pace and opportunity.

Research concept: Pace & Minutes

🏒 Hockey

The puck moves fast. A deflection, penalty or turnover can turn a controlled game into a scramble. Shot quality matters more than raw volume alone.

Research concept: Storm Risk

⚽ Soccer

A huge field hides off-ball movement. One counterattack, red card or penalty can transform a match. Possession does not guarantee safety.

Research concept: Storm Risk

Calm water is not always safe water

Soccer and hockey can turn like the ocean. A team may look comfortable until a sudden high-impact play changes everything. Our proposed Storm Risk framework asks whether the conditions supporting a lead are deteriorating—not merely who is ahead.

The number of players involved is a useful way to think about complexity, but it does not prove one sport is inherently easier or safer to predict. Broadcast visibility, scoring frequency, data quality and market price all matter.

Case study: Brunson's 25-point pace

During a Knicks–Bucks game, we followed Jalen Brunson's reported scoring as the game developed. This is an illustrative, user-reported sequence, not a verified historical feed.

Game checkpointPointsWhat changed
Halftime10Behind a simple even-paced target
Q3 · 3:00 left16Needed nine more; playing time uncertain
Q3 · 1:00 left20Scoring accelerated
End of Q321Only four more needed
Early Q423Two more needed; not yet settled

At 21 points through 36 elapsed game minutes, a simple straight-line extrapolation suggested 28 points by the end of regulation. But that estimate ignored substitutions, actual minutes played and defensive changes. The observer also noticed Milwaukee cutting into New York's lead, potentially increasing the need to keep Brunson playing.

The lesson isn't “always enter early.” An earlier entry may pay more because it carries more uncertainty. The question is whether new information justifies the price and the additional exposure.

Our decision discipline

ADD

Evidence strengthens the thesis, the price offers value, and total exposure stays within a predefined limit.

HOLD

The original thesis remains supported; no compelling new reason to change.

REDUCE

New warnings, deteriorating conditions or excessive exposure justify cutting risk.

EXIT

The thesis materially breaks and an executable exit is preferable to remaining exposed.

These are research labels for human decisions—not trade signals, instructions or automated execution.

What we're building next

We want the radar to combine sport-specific observations, reliable source timestamps, transparent scoring projections, estimated fair value, and position-risk awareness. We will test ideas against historical data before treating them as predictive indicators.

What exists today versus what's proposed: The current workbench supports manual market scanning and limited sport research. The Collapse Indicator, Storm Risk, live player-prop projections and decision labels described here are proposed capabilities, not features already deployed.

Our mission: Software should make people's lives better, not worse. That includes being clear about uncertainty and never claiming certainty where there is none.
